Johnson–Cook only shows its real character when it is coupled with temperature. In fast deformation about ninety percent of the plastic work becomes heat – the Taylor–Quinney coefficient – and above roughly ten to a hundred per second there is no time for the heat to flow away. The deformation is adiabatic. The chart: AISI ten forty-five at ten thousand per second. The grey curve is isothermal at 20 degrees. The red curve includes the temperature rise from plastic work – about 258 kelvin at a strain of one – and the thermal bracket pulls the curve down. Hardening and softening compete; where softening wins, deformation localises into adiabatic shear bands. That is exactly the mechanism of segmented chips in machining of hardened steels and titanium. Two consequences for practice: run machining and impact simulations as coupled thermo-mechanical analyses with density, specific heat and conductivity; and never fit Johnson–Cook to Hopkinson-bar curves as if they were isothermal.
