What does n actually mean? Differentiate Hollomon's law: the hardening rate is n times stress over strain. So n tells how strongly the material hardens relative to its current stress. Now the tensile test. The force is stress times the current area. As the specimen stretches, the area falls and the stress rises. The force reaches its maximum – and necking starts – when the hardening rate drops to the stress itself. That is Considère's criterion. Insert Hollomon's law and you get a beautiful result: the uniform true strain equals n. The chart shows it: the red true-stress curve and the blue hardening-rate curve cross exactly at strain 0.24, where the grey engineering curve – force over original area – has its maximum. So n is a measure of how long the material spreads deformation before it localises. Austenitic stainless steel with n around 0.45 stretches a lot and hardens strongly; a cold-worked or high-strength steel with n of 0.1 necks early. And it gives you a free check: the n you fit should be close to the uniform elongation of the tensile test.
