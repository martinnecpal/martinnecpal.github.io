Where do these curves come from? Four tests. The tensile test is the standard, but necking limits it to strains of 0.2 or 0.3 – useless on its own for forging, where strains reach 2 or 3. Compression – upsetting a cylinder – reaches strains around one. Its weakness is friction: the specimen barrels, the stress is no longer uniaxial and the measured force must be corrected, often by inverse FEM in DEFORM itself. The Gleeble – the photo – is a thermomechanical simulator. It heats the specimen by direct resistance and compresses it at controlled temperature and strain rate; this is how most hot flow curves in libraries are measured. There is a short Gleeble video linked under the picture if you want to see it. For strain rates of thousands per second – machining, impact – we use the split Hopkinson pressure bar, the sketch on the right: a striker hits a bar, the wave passes through a small specimen, and strain gauges give stress and strain rate. The table says what each test is good for. Torsion I add for completeness: it reaches enormous strains and is used for hot rolling and recrystallisation studies.
