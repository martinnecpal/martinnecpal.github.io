The second exponent is m, the strain-rate sensitivity: the slope of log stress against log strain rate. The simplest way to measure it is a jump test: deform at one rate, suddenly increase it ten times, and read the step in stress. The chart shows it for a cold steel with m of 0.012 – about three percent stress increase for a tenfold rate change. The table gives typical values. Cold metals have m close to zero – that is why the strain-rate term is often dropped in cold forging. In warm forming m rises to several percent, hot forming 0.1 to 0.2, and superplastic alloys reach 0.5 – a tenfold faster deformation then needs three times the stress. Physically, positive m stabilises the flow: where a neck starts, the local strain rate rises, the stress needed rises, and the neck is slowed down. That is the secret of superplastic elongations of several hundred percent. One warning for steels between about 150 and 350 degrees: dynamic strain ageing makes m negative and the flow serrated – the classical blue brittleness range. Avoid warm forming of steel there.
