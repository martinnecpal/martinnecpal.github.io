Kinematic hardening is the other extreme: the yield surface keeps its size but moves, the centre shifted by the back stress alpha. Why would that matter? Look at the chart. We pull the material to 5 percent strain, then reverse. With isotropic hardening it yields again in compression only at the same magnitude it reached in tension. With kinematic hardening the elastic range stays two times sigma zero wide, so it yields in compression much earlier. That early reverse yielding is the Bauschinger effect, and real metals show it. In sheet forming the metal bends and unbends over die radii and drawbeads – exactly a load reversal. Ignore the Bauschinger effect and springback predictions are off. The simplest laws are Prager and Ziegler; Armstrong–Frederick adds saturation. In practice a mixed rule – part isotropic, part kinematic – is selected in the hardening rule dropdown.
