Now the mathematics, in four steps. Step one: from the Arrhenius law Zerilli and Armstrong derived that the thermal stress decays exponentially with temperature, with an exponent beta that itself depends on the logarithm of strain rate. The amplitude contains the Taylor factor M, the activation energy at absolute zero, the Burgers vector b and the activation area A – the area a dislocation sweeps during one activation event. Step two, BCC metals. The obstacle is the lattice itself – the Peierls barrier – and its size does not change with strain. So A is constant, and the thermal term does not depend on strain. Strain hardening appears as a separate, athermal power-law term C5 times strain to the n. This is the BCC form. Step three, FCC metals. The obstacles are forest dislocations. Their density grows linearly with strain, so the spacing – and the activation area – falls with the square root of strain. Put A proportional to strain to the minus one half into step one, and you get the FCC form: square root of strain multiplied by the exponential. Strain and temperature are now coupled in one term. Step four: C0 collects the athermal stress and the Hall–Petch grain-size term. On the right, the meaning and unit of each constant. Remember the last line: temperature in kelvin, strain rate in per second.
