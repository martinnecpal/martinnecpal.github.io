The sinh law gives one number – a peak or steady-state stress. FEM needs the whole curve. Two approaches are common. The first is purely mathematical: the strain-compensated Arrhenius model. You repeat the calibration at many strains – say every 0.05 from 0.05 to 0.8 – and fit each constant, alpha, n, Q and ln A, with a polynomial in strain. Lin and co-workers popularised this in two thousand and eight for 42CrMo steel, and it typically fits with three to six percent error. The price: twenty to thirty coefficients with no physical meaning, and nonsense outside the fitted range of strain. The second approach uses physics: the chart shows the typical shape. The curve rises to a peak, dynamic recrystallisation starts at the critical strain, about 0.8 of the peak strain, and the stress falls to a steady state. The higher Z, the higher the peak and the later it comes. These curves are illustrative, but the trends are exactly what DEFORM's microstructure models use.
