How do you get the constants? First the test matrix: quasi-static tests and Hopkinson-bar tests at several temperatures – from liquid nitrogen up to about half the melting point. Second, the athermal part: the strain-hardening exponent and C5 come from the hardening increments on a log–log plot; C0 from grain size or from the plateau at high temperature, where the thermal part has gone. Third – the key trick – take the logarithm of what remains. The logarithm of the thermal stress is linear in temperature, with the intercept ln C1 and a slope that depends on strain rate. The chart shows these straight lines for three strain rates, with synthetic test points scattered around them. Fourth, plot the slopes against the logarithm of strain rate: the intercept gives C3, the slope C4. Finally check the fit with an error measure and, ideally, by simulating an independent test. A warning: at Hopkinson-bar rates the specimen heats up adiabatically, so the measured curve is not isothermal – correct for that before fitting.
