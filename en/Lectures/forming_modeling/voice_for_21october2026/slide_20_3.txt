Historically, two laws were used. At low stresses – creep conditions – Z is proportional to stress to a power n prime. At high stresses – fast hot working – Z grows exponentially with stress. Each works in its own range and fails in the other; the transition is known in creep literature as power-law breakdown. Garofalo in nineteen sixty-three, and then Sellars and Tegart in nineteen sixty-six for hot working, proposed the hyperbolic sine. For small arguments sinh x is about x, so the law becomes a power law. For large arguments sinh x is about e to the x over two, so it becomes exponential. The chart shows this with one set of constants: the red sinh curve follows the blue power law at low stress and the orange exponential at high stress, and switches over in the grey band. The constants: n between about 3 and 6, alpha – the stress multiplier – around 0.01 per megapascal for steels and larger for aluminium alloys. Alpha is usually taken as beta divided by n prime, which already tells you how it is measured.
