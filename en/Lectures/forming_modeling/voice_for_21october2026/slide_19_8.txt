Where is the model used? The first line is how it was validated: the Taylor impact test. A cylinder is fired at a rigid anvil at 100 to 300 metres per second; it shortens and forms a mushroom. One test covers a whole range of strains, strain rates and temperatures, so comparing the final shape with an FEM simulation checks the model at once. Zerilli and Armstrong did exactly this for copper and iron. Then defence applications – ballistic penetrators and explosively formed projectiles made of iron, tantalum or tungsten, all BCC metals with a strong rate effect. For our field, the most relevant are high-speed machining, where studies have compared ZA and Johnson–Cook for chip formation in steels, and high-speed forming – electromagnetic forming, explosive forming, hammer forging of ferritic materials. Also cold heading and crash simulation, where the BCC-versus-FCC difference matters. Finally, hot forging and rolling, but only through the modified ZA, used as a semi-empirical fit of hot compression data.
