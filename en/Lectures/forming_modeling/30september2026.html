Motivation Why simulate forming?

From press-shop trials to virtual try-out

  • Forging dies and stamping tools cost weeks of work and tens of thousands of euros; every modification adds more
  • Most of a product's cost is fixed at the design stage, long before production starts
  • Simulation moves the iterations into the computer: change the preform, die radius or lubricant and run the calculation again
  • Result: fewer tool modifications, faster production ramp-up, less material (flash, scrap)

The iteration loop moves from the press shop into the computer

[0:02–0:05] Ask the students: how many tool trials would you expect for a new forging without simulation? Answer from practice: several, each taking weeks. Show the dashed loops: red means hours, grey means weeks.
Motivation What the software tells us

Questions that forming simulation answers

  • Will the die cavity fill? — underfill, laps, folds, piping
  • What press do I need? — force and energy versus stroke
  • Will the part crack or neck? — damage, FLD, thinning
  • Will it keep its shape? — springback, warpage, distortion
  • What will the tool life be? — stress, wear, temperature
  • What is inside? — grain size, phases, hardness

Analytical methods Overview

Classical analytical methods of plasticity theory

[0:10–0:13] Quick map. The upper bound always overestimates the force — which is safe when choosing a press. Slip lines give exact solutions, but only for plane strain and perfect plasticity.

Slab method: upsetting of a cylinder

$$\frac{dp}{dr} = -\frac{2\mu\,p}{h} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; p(r)=\sigma_f\, e^{\,2\mu (R-r)/h}$$
$$F=\int_0^R p(r)\,2\pi r\,dr, \qquad R = R_0\sqrt{h_0/h}$$
  • \(p\) contact pressure, \(\sigma_f\) flow stress, \(\mu\) Coulomb friction
  • \(h, R\) current height and radius (volume constancy)
  • Pressure peaks in the centre — the friction hill
  • Larger \(\mu\) or smaller \(h\) ⇒ steeper hill ⇒ higher force

Friction hill — contact pressure along the radius (schematic)

[0:13–0:17] If there is time, derive the equilibrium on the board. Stress that you will simulate exactly this upsetting test in DEFORM in the lab — the analytical force is the first check of the FEM result.

Drawing and extrusion: closed-form results

Sachs (slab method) — wire drawing stress

$$\sigma_d=\sigma_f\,\frac{1+B}{B}\left[1-\left(\frac{A_1}{A_0}\right)^{B}\right],\; B=\mu\cot\alpha$$
  • \(\alpha\) die semi-angle, \(A_0, A_1\) cross-section before and after
  • Condition: \(\sigma_d \le \sigma_f\) ⇒ maximum reduction per pass

Upper bound — extrusion pressure

$$\frac{p}{\sigma_f}\approx \underbrace{\ln R_{ext}}_{\text{ideal}}+\underbrace{\mu\cot\alpha\,\ln R_{ext}}_{\text{friction}}+\underbrace{\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\!\left(\tfrac{\alpha}{\sin^2\alpha}-\cot\alpha\right)}_{\text{redundant (shear)}}$$
  • \(R_{ext}=A_0/A_1\) extrusion ratio, \(\alpha\) die semi-angle
  • The shear term grows with α, the friction term falls ⇒ optimum die angle → optimisation problem
Didactic form — simplified (Avitzur-type upper bound for a conical die); see the full derivation in Hosford & Caddell and Avitzur before using the numbers.
[0:17–0:19] What matters is not the formula itself but its structure: one term grows with the die angle, the other falls — so an optimum exists. This turns the analytical model into an optimisation problem, which leads us to the solvers on the next slide.

# Upsetting force – slab method (Python)
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad
sf, mu = 250.0, 0.2          # MPa, -
R0, h0 = 10.0, 30.0          # mm
for h in np.linspace(30, 10, 5):
    R = R0*np.sqrt(h0/h)     # volume constancy
    p = lambda r: sf*np.exp(2*mu*(R-r)/h)
    F = quad(lambda r: p(r)*2*np.pi*r, 0, R)[0]
    print(f"h={h:5.1f} mm  F={F/1e3:7.1f} kN")

Software Overview

Two groups of forming software

Dedicated process-oriented

  • Templates: presses, tools, draw beads, passes
  • For the process engineer, not the FEM specialist
  • Sheet: Ansys Forming, AutoForm, PAM-STAMP
  • Bulk: Simufact, DEFORM, QForm, FORGE
  • Plastics: Moldflow, Moldex3D

General-purpose nonlinear FEM

  • The user builds everything: parts, contacts, steps
  • Maximum flexibility, user subroutines (UMAT)
  • Abaqus, LS-DYNA, Ansys Mechanical, MSC Marc
  • Research, teaching, unusual processes
Solver typePrincipleWhere
Implicit (static)Equilibrium in every increment, large stepsDEFORM, Abaqus/Standard, springback
Explicit (dynamic)Small stable time steps, robust contactLS-DYNA (Ansys Forming), Abaqus/Explicit
Finite volumes (Euler)Fixed mesh, material flows through itSimufact FV, Moldflow solvers
[0:25–0:28] Give the students two "drawers" to sort every program into. Explicit vs. implicit solver is a common exam question.

Every program follows the same workflow

1. Geometry — import from CAD (STEP, IGES, STL), symmetry
↓
2. Material — flow curve, thermal data, damage
↓
3. Mesh — element type, size, remeshing
↓
4. Boundary conditions — friction, heat transfer, tool motion
↓
5. Solution — solver, time steps, stopping criteria
↓
6. Evaluation — fields, curves, defects, report
  • Steps 1–4 = preprocessing (lecture 4)
  • Step 6 = postprocessing (lectures 5–6)
  • Programs differ mainly in how much of steps 1–4 their templates automate
  • "Garbage in, garbage out": the quality of the answer is set by the flow curve and the friction coefficient

The DEFORM solver: rigid-viscoplastic formulation

$$\pi=\int_V \bar{\sigma}\,\dot{\bar{\varepsilon}}\,dV+\frac{K}{2}\int_V \dot{\varepsilon}_v^{\,2}\,dV-\int_{S_F} F_i\,u_i\,dS \;\;\rightarrow\;\; \delta\pi = 0$$
  • \(\bar\sigma, \dot{\bar\varepsilon}\) effective stress and strain rate · \(K\) penalty constant (incompressibility) · \(F_i\) surface tractions
  • The unknowns are velocities → Newton-Raphson / direct iteration in every step (implicit, updated Lagrangian)
  • Elastic strains are neglected — suited to large plastic flow (forging); elasto-plastic option for springback, residual stresses, tools
  • ALE and steady-state solvers for ring rolling, shape rolling, extrusion, tube piercing

Source: Kobayashi, Oh & Altan (1989); DEFORM-3D brochure

[1:12–1:15] This is the flow formulation from ALPID. Link to Part I: minimising a power functional — the same idea as the upper bound method, but with a discretised velocity field instead of a guessed one.

Automatic remeshing and multiple operations

  • The Lagrangian mesh deforms with the material → automatic remeshing when an element degenerates or penetrates the tool
  • Tetrahedra (3D), quadrilaterals (2D), optionally hexahedra; mesh windows refine critical regions
  • Each remeshing interpolates state variables → keep the number of remeshings reasonable (volume loss)

Normalised Cockcroft–Latham criterion

$$D=\int_0^{\bar\varepsilon_f}\frac{\sigma_1}{\bar\sigma}\,d\bar\varepsilon \;\ge\; C$$

Archard tool wear

$$w=\int K\,\frac{p\,v}{H^{\,a}}\,dt$$

σ₁ maximum principal stress · p pressure · v sliding velocity · H tool hardness

[1:23–1:26] Cockcroft–Latham: chevron cracks in extrusion, surface cracks in upsetting. Archard: wear grows with pressure and sliding, falls with tool hardness — hot tools soften, so temperature matters.

Summary

  • Analytical methods (slab method, upper bound, slip lines) give a quick force estimate and are the first check of every FEM result
  • Dedicated software automates the process: Ansys Forming (stamping), Simufact (bulk + joining), Moldflow (polymers)
  • General-purpose FEM (Abaqus) is flexible, but the user builds everything
  • DEFORM: implicit rigid-viscoplastic FEM, automatic remeshing, damage, tool wear, microstructure — our tool for the lab sessions
  • Special software goes beyond the limits of FEM: FVM, SPH, DEM, inverse and multiscale methods, AI surrogate models
